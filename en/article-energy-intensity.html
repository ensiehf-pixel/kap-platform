Energy intensity in Iran and the impact of the electricity shortfall on GDP

📅 August 2026 ⏱ 9 min read ✍️ KAP Economic & Strategic Studies

'Energy intensity' measures how much energy is used to create one unit of wealth, or GDP. World Bank and International Energy Agency (IEA) figures reveal a worrying fact: Iran's energy intensity is around 2.5 to 3 times the global average and more than twice that of advanced industrial economies. Put simply, producing one dollar of value added in Iran's economy wastes 2 to 3 times more fuel and electricity than global standards.

The vicious cycle of energy shortfall and slowing growth

When the gap between electricity supply and demand peaks, the burden of power cuts inevitably falls on the productive sector — factories, industrial parks, petrochemical and steel plants. The direct consequences are:

  • Lower output and exports: direct losses from stopped production lines, costing the country's trade balance billions of dollars a year.
  • Wear on equipment and process lines: sudden power cuts seriously damage furnaces, compressors and industrial control systems.
  • Lower labour productivity and higher overheads: workers forced to stop while fixed costs keep running.
'Every 1,000 MW of shortfall that leads to industrial power cuts directly shrinks national output and pushes up the price of basic goods.'

Is building new power plants the only answer?

Closing the current 20,000 MW gap with new plants would need more than $15 billion of foreign-currency investment and billions of cubic metres of extra natural gas — resources that are far from easy to secure under sanctions and falling gas-field pressure. And every large plant takes at least 3 to 5 years to build.

Leading countries have shown that the most economical, fastest and most sustainable solution is 'demand-side management and optimisation powered by the digital economy' .

The KAP platform: rescuing productivity with smart data

By analysing real-time data from ESA devices and consuming equipment, the KAP platform pinpoints exactly where energy is wasted:

  • Hidden and standby loads outside working hours: hundreds of megawatts are spent at night and on holidays in universities, offices and factories on cooling systems and idle equipment.
  • Smart shifting of process loads: scheduling pumps, mills and loading to the grid's off-peak hours without disrupting production plans.
  • Economic rewards for saving: transparent financial incentives for every kilowatt-hour of load reduced during grid emergencies.

Conclusion: where AI meets energy economics

With less than 5% of the cost of building a physical power plant, data-driven platforms like KAP can harness several thousand megawatts of flexible load in under six months and protect the country's production capacity — directly protecting GDP and people's livelihoods.
